Quick check

\(X\sim\) Uniform{−2, −1, 0, 1, 2} and \(Y=X^2\). Range of \(Y\) is {0, 1, 4}; \(P(Y=1)=P(X=-1)+P(X=1)=2/5\).

01L1.1–L1.7
Joint, marginal, conditional

1. Joint PMF of two random variables

Definition

For discrete \(X, Y\) defined on the same experiment, the joint PMF is \(f_{XY}(t_1,t_2)=P(X=t_1,\ Y=t_2)\) for \(t_1\in T_X,\ t_2\in T_Y\). Every entry is ≥ 0 and all entries add up to 1.

Write it as a table: one row per value of one variable, one column per value of the other. Any probability about \((X,Y)\) is a sum of cells: \(P(X+Y=3)\) is the sum of the cells with \(t_1+t_2=3\).

Example

A fair coin is tossed twice. \(X\) = heads on toss 1, \(Y\) = total heads.

\(f_{XY}(1,0)=0\): you cannot have 0 heads in total after a head on toss 1. Zeros like this are what make variables dependent.

  • Unknown entries (a, b, k): use "sum of all cells = 1" plus whatever extra condition is given (a marginal, a conditional, or \(a=b/2\)).
  • Formula-type joint PMFs such as \(f_{XY}(i,j)=\frac{i+2j}{k}\) on a grid: fill the table, add it up, set it equal to 1 to get k.
  • Counting experiments (cards, balls without replacement): \(f_{XY}(x,y)=\frac{\binom{a}{x}\binom{b}{y}\binom{\text{rest}}{n-x-y}}{\binom{N}{n}}\). The "rest" factor is the one people forget.

2. Marginal PMF

Definition

\(f_X(t_1)=\sum_{t_2\in T_Y} f_{XY}(t_1,t_2)\) (add along the row), and \(f_Y(t_2)=\sum_{t_1\in T_X} f_{XY}(t_1,t_2)\) (add down the column).

  • The marginal is an ordinary PMF: it adds up to 1.
  • Different joint PMFs can have the same marginals. The marginals alone never tell you the joint (unless the variables are independent).
  • In the coin example: \(f_X=(1/2,1/2)\), \(f_Y=(1/4,1/2,1/4)\).

3. Conditional distribution

Definition

For \(t_1\) with \(f_X(t_1)>0\): \(f_{Y\mid X=t_1}(t_2)=\dfrac{f_{XY}(t_1,t_2)}{f_X(t_1)}\). Take the row of \(t_1\) and divide it by its own total.

  • A conditional PMF adds up to 1 over \(t_2\): \(\sum_{t_2} f_{Y\mid X=t_1}(t_2)=1\).
  • Range of \((Y\mid X=t_1)\) = the values of \(t_2\) whose cell in that row is non-zero. It can be smaller than \(T_Y\).
  • Factorisation: \(f_{XY}(t_1,t_2)=f_{Y\mid X=t_1}(t_2)\,f_X(t_1)=f_{X\mid Y=t_2}(t_1)\,f_Y(t_2)\). Use it to build a joint PMF from a two-stage experiment.
  • Conditioning on an event works the same way: \(P(X=t\mid A)=P(X=t, A)/P(A)\).
Two-stage example

Pick a card. If it is a face card (12 of 52) draw 2 balls from bag A, otherwise 3 balls from bag B. \(Y\) = number of red balls. Then \(f_Y(1)=P(\text{face})\,P(Y=1\mid\text{face})+P(\text{not face})\,P(Y=1\mid\text{not face})\), each conditional being hypergeometric. This "total probability over the first stage" is a standard quiz move.

4. Poisson splitting (the most repeated idea in Week 1)

Result

If \(N\sim\) Poisson(λ) and, given \(N=n\), each of the n items independently has a property with probability p, so that \((X\mid N=n)\sim\) Binomial(n, p), then \[f_{NX}(n,k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^n}{n!}\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad X\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda p).\] Also \(N-X\sim\) Poisson(λ(1−p)), and \(X\) and \(N-X\) are independent.

  • \(f_{NX}(n,k)=0\) when \(k>n\) (you cannot have more spam emails than emails).
  • Joint probability of "exactly b boys and g girls": \(P(B=b,G=g)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{b+g}}{b!\,g!}p^g(1-p)^b\). With p = 0.5 this is \(\frac{e^{-\lambda}(\lambda/2)^{b+g}}{b!\,g!}\).
  • \(E[X]=\lambda p\) (useful in Week 3 questions).

5. Three or more random variables

  • Joint PMF \(f_{X_1X_2X_3}(t_1,t_2,t_3)=P(X_1=t_1,X_2=t_2,X_3=t_3)\), usually given as a list of rows (t₁, t₂, t₃, prob).
  • Marginalise by summing out the variables you do not want: \(f_{X_1X_2}(t_1,t_2)=\sum_{t_3}f_{X_1X_2X_3}(t_1,t_2,t_3)\); \(f_{X_1}(t_1)=\sum_{t_2,t_3}f(t_1,t_2,t_3)\).
  • Condition on any subset: \(f_{X_1\mid X_2=a,X_3=b}(t)=\dfrac{f_{X_1X_2X_3}(t,a,b)}{f_{X_2X_3}(a,b)}\); \(P(X_3=1,X_4=1\mid X_1=1,X_2=1)=\dfrac{f(1,1,1,1)}{f_{X_1X_2}(1,1)}\).
  • Chain rule (any order): \(f_{X_1X_2X_3}=f_{X_1\mid X_2,X_3}\cdot f_{X_2\mid X_3}\cdot f_{X_3}\). Typical quiz item: given three of these four numbers, find the fourth.
  • \(f_{XYZ}=f_{X\mid Y,Z}\,f_{YZ}\) is always true; \(f_{X\mid Y,Z}\,f_Y\,f_Z\) is true only under independence of Y and Z.
Chain-rule example

\(f_{X_1X_2X_3}(0,1,2)=?\) given \(f_{X_3}(2)=0.4\), \(f_{X_2\mid X_3=2}(1)=0.5\), \(f_{X_1\mid X_2=1,X_3=2}(0)=0.25\).

Show working

\(0.25\times0.5\times0.4=0.05\).

Watch out

In "three cards from a deck, X kings, Y jacks, Z queens", the remaining cards are the 40 non-K/J/Q cards. \(P(X=1,Y=0,Z=1)=\frac{\binom41\binom41\binom{40}1}{\binom{52}3}\).

02L2.1–L2.11
Independence, functions

1. Independence of two random variables

Definition

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if \(f_{XY}(t_1,t_2)=f_X(t_1)\,f_Y(t_2)\) for every \(t_1\in T_X,\ t_2\in T_Y\). Equivalently \(f_{Y\mid X=t_1}(t_2)=f_Y(t_2)\) for every \(t_1\).

  • One failing cell is enough to prove dependence. One matching cell proves nothing.
  • Zero test: if some cell is 0 while its row total and column total are both non-zero, \(X\) and \(Y\) are dependent. Fastest check in an exam.
  • Row test: independent ⇔ every row of the table is a multiple of every other row (all rows proportional to the column totals).
  • Joint PMF that factors as \(g(x)h(y)\) on a rectangular range (e.g. \(\frac{9}{16\cdot 4^{x+y}}=\frac{3}{4^{x+1}}\cdot\frac{3}{4^{y+1}}\)) ⇒ independent. If the range is not a rectangle (e.g. \(x\lt y\)) they are dependent.
  • Two dice: \(X\) = die A and \(Y\) = number of sixes are dependent (\(X=6\) forces \(Y\ge1\)). Heads and tails in the same tosses are dependent (\(Y=n-X\)).

2. Many random variables, i.i.d.

  • \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are independent if the joint PMF is the product of all n marginals, for every combination of values. Then every subset is independent too, and conditioning on some of them does not change the others: \(f_{X_1X_2\mid X_3=t}(a,b)=f_{X_1}(a)f_{X_2}(b)\).
  • Pairwise independence does not imply independence of all three (even-parity example: \(X_3=X_1\oplus X_2\)).
  • i.i.d. = independent and identically distributed (same PMF). Repeated tosses, die throws, samples.
  • "All n samples exceed c": \(P(X_1>c,\dots,X_n>c)=P(X>c)^n\). For 10 i.i.d. Geometric(0.2), \(P(\text{all}>10)=(0.8^{10})^{10}=0.8^{100}\).
  • "Exactly one sample equals a and exactly one equals b" among n i.i.d.: choose positions, \(n(n-1)\,P(a)P(b)\,[1-P(a)-P(b)]^{n-2}\).
Memoryless property of Geometric

\(P(X>m+n\mid X>m)=P(X>n)=(1-p)^n\). "No bug in the first 3 runs; probability the first bug comes after run 5" = \(P(X>5\mid X>3)=(1-p)^2\).

3. Functions of random variables

  • \(Z=g(X_1,\dots,X_n)\): for each value z, \(f_Z(z)=\sum f_{X_1\dots X_n}(t_1,\dots,t_n)\) over all tuples with \(g(t_1,\dots,t_n)=z\).
  • Work it as a table: list each tuple with its probability and its g-value, then group by g-value. The range is the set of g-values with non-zero probability.
  • Joint PMF of two functions \((g,h)\): group by the pair of values. Same method.
  • One-to-one functions keep the probabilities and just relabel; many-to-one functions merge probabilities.

4. Sum of two random variables

Convolution

\(P(X+Y=z)=\sum_{x}f_{XY}(x,\,z-x)\). If independent: \(f_Z(z)=\sum_x f_X(x)\,f_Y(z-x)\).

  • Poisson + Poisson: independent \(X\sim\)Poisson(λ₁), \(Y\sim\)Poisson(λ₂) ⇒ \(X+Y\sim\)Poisson(λ₁+λ₂), and \((X\mid X+Y=n)\sim\) Binomial\((n,\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2})\).
  • Binomial + Binomial (same p): \(X_1+X_2\sim\)Binomial(n₁+n₂, p), and \((X_1\mid X_1+X_2=m)\sim\) HyperGeo(n₁+n₂, n₁, m), which does not depend on p.
  • Sum of n i.i.d. Bernoulli(p) = Binomial(n, p). Given the sum is k, each \(X_i\) is 1 with probability k/n: \(P(X_1=0\mid Y=8)=2/10\) when n = 10.
  • Two i.i.d. Geometric(p): \(P(X+Y=n)=(n-1)p^2(1-p)^{n-2}\) for n ≥ 2, and \((X\mid X+Y=n)\) is uniform on {1, …, n−1}, whatever p is. So \(P(X=6\mid X+Y=10)=1/9\).
  • Difference of i.i.d. Geometric(p): \(P(X-Y=k)=\frac{p(1-p)^{k}}{2-p}\) for k ≥ 1 (and \(\frac{p}{2-p}\) for k = 0).
  • Two fair dice: \(P(X+Y=s)=\frac{6-|s-7|}{36}\); \(P(X-Y=d)=\frac{6-|d|}{36}\).

5. Maximum and minimum

CDF trick

For independent \(X_1,\dots,X_n\): \(P(\max\le k)=\prod_i P(X_i\le k)\) and \(P(\min\ge k)=\prod_i P(X_i\ge k)\). For i.i.d. with CDF F: \(F_{\max}(k)=F(k)^n\), \(F_{\min}(k)=1-\big(1-F(k)\big)^n\) (integer-valued, with \(P(X\gt k)=1-F(k)\)).

  • PMF of the max (integer values): \(P(\max=k)=F(k)^n-F(k-1)^n\). Two dice: \(P(\max=k)=\frac{k^2-(k-1)^2}{36}=\frac{2k-1}{36}\).
  • PMF of the min: \(P(\min=k)=P(\min\ge k)-P(\min\ge k+1)\). Two dice: \(P(\min=k)=\frac{(7-k)^2-(6-k)^2}{36}=\frac{13-2k}{36}\).
  • Min of independent Geometrics is Geometric: \(\min(X,Y)\sim\) Geometric\((1-(1-p_1)(1-p_2))\).
  • Max of Bernoullis: \(P(\max=0)=\prod P(X_i=0)\). Min of Bernoullis: \(P(\min=1)=\prod P(X_i=1)\).
  • i.i.d. Uniform{1,…,m}: \(P(\min(X,Y)=k)=\frac{(m-k+1)^2-(m-k)^2}{m^2}\).
Example

\(X_1,\dots,X_5\) i.i.d. Binomial(4, 1/4). \(P(X\le 2)=1-P(3)-P(4)=1-\frac{12}{256}-\frac1{256}=\frac{243}{256}\).

Show working

\(P(\max\le2)=(243/256)^5\). \(P(X\ge 3)=13/256\), so \(P(\min\le 2)=1-P(\text{all}\ge3)=1-(13/256)^5\).

03L3.1–L3.7
E, Var, Cov, ρ, bounds

1. Expected value

Definition

\(E[X]=\sum_{t\in T_X} t\,f_X(t)\). For a function: \(E[g(X)]=\sum_t g(t)f_X(t)\) and \(E[g(X,Y)]=\sum_{t_1,t_2}g(t_1,t_2)\,f_{XY}(t_1,t_2)\). You never need the PMF of \(g(X,Y)\) itself.

  • Linearity (always true, even for dependent variables): \(E[aX+bY+c]=aE[X]+bE[Y]+c\).
  • \(E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]\) if X and Y are independent. The converse is false.
  • \(X\ge0\) ⇒ \(E[X]\ge0\). A constant has \(E[c]=c\).
  • \(E[X^2]\ne(E[X])^2\) in general (the gap is the variance).
Indicator trick

Write a count as a sum of 0/1 indicators and add their means: \(E[\#]=\sum P(\text{event}_i)\). Hat matching: n people, each gets own hat with prob 1/n, so \(E=n\cdot\frac1n=1\). Six balls removed from balls 1–10: each removed ball has mean 5.5, so \(E[\text{sum}]=6\times5.5=33\).

  • Lottery / coupon expected prize: buying 2 of 600 tickets where the prizes total ₹1800: each ticket's mean prize is 1800/600 = 3, so \(E=2\times3=6\), i.e. ₹6. Without-replacement draws still add means.
  • Random number of customers: if \(N\sim\)Poisson(λ) customers each spend an average of m, expected total = λ·m. Expected buyers when each buys with prob p: λp.
  • Investments: a ₹2000 share going ×1.5 w.p. 0.3 or ×0.5 w.p. 0.7 has expected value 2000(0.45+0.35) = 1600. Independent day-to-day factors multiply their means.

2. Variance and standard deviation

Definition

\(\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2\), \(\mathrm{SD}(X)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}\).

  • \(\mathrm{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)\), \(\mathrm{SD}(aX+b)=|a|\,\mathrm{SD}(X)\). Adding a constant changes nothing.
  • \(\mathrm{Var}(X\pm Y)=\mathrm{Var}X+\mathrm{Var}Y\pm2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\). If independent the covariance term is 0, so \(\mathrm{Var}(X-Y)=\mathrm{Var}X+\mathrm{Var}Y\) (variances add, never subtract).
  • Independent \(X, Y\): \(\mathrm{Var}(XY)=E[X^2]E[Y^2]-(E[X]E[Y])^2\), where \(E[X^2]=\mathrm{Var}X+(E X)^2\).
  • \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\tfrac12E[(X-X')^2]\) for an i.i.d. copy \(X'\).
  • Standardised variable \(Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\) has mean 0 and variance 1.
Example

\(X\) takes 0, 2, 4 with probabilities 1/2, 1/3, 1/6. \(E[X]=\frac23+\frac46=\frac43\); \(E[X^2]=\frac43+\frac{16}6=4\); \(\mathrm{Var}=4-\frac{16}9=\frac{20}9\).

Also: E[(X − 2)²]

\(E[(X-2)^2]=\frac12\cdot4+\frac13\cdot0+\frac16\cdot4=\frac83\). Check: \(\mathrm{Var}+(\mu-2)^2=\frac{20}9+\frac49=\frac{24}9=\frac83\).

3. Covariance

Definition

\(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-E X)(Y-E Y)]=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\).

  • \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,X)=\mathrm{Var}(X)\); \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,c)=0\); symmetric.
  • Bilinear: \(\mathrm{Cov}(aX+bY,\,cZ+dW)=ac\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Z)+ad\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,W)+bc\,\mathrm{Cov}(Y,Z)+bd\,\mathrm{Cov}(Y,W)\). Constants added inside disappear.
  • Independent ⇒ Cov = 0. Cov = 0 does not imply independent (e.g. X uniform on {−1, 0, 1} and Y = X²).
  • \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}X+\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\).
Example

Cov(X,Y) = 4, Cov(X,Z) = 1, Cov(Y,Z) = 3, Var(Y) = 6. Find Cov(2X + 3Y, Y − Z).

Show working

\(2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)-2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Z)+3\mathrm{Var}(Y)-3\mathrm{Cov}(Y,Z)=8-2+18-9=15\).

4. Correlation coefficient

Definition

\(\rho(X,Y)=\dfrac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\mathrm{SD}(X)\,\mathrm{SD}(Y)}\), always in \([-1,1]\).

  • \(\rho=\pm1\) exactly when \(Y=aX+b\) (sign of a). Two balls from 5 white + 5 red: X red + Y white = 2, so \(\rho(X,Y)=-1\).
  • \(\rho(aX+b,\,cY+d)=\pm\rho(X,Y)\), sign of ac. Scaling never changes the size of ρ.
  • ρ is the covariance of the standardised variables: \(\mathrm{Cov}\!\big(\frac{X-\mu_X}{\sigma_X},\frac{Y-\mu_Y}{\sigma_Y}\big)=\rho\). If X and Y are independent this is 0.
  • Joint PMF that factors (e.g. \(\frac{(x+1)(y+1)}{27}\)) ⇒ independent ⇒ ρ = 0.

5. Bounds from mean and variance

Markov

If \(X\ge0\) with mean μ, then for c > 0: \(P(X\ge c)\le \dfrac{\mu}{c}\).

Chebyshev

If X has mean μ and variance σ², then \(P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le\dfrac1{k^2}\), i.e. \(P(|X-\mu|\ge c)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{c^2}\). Lower bound: \(P(|X-\mu|\lt c)\ge1-\dfrac{\sigma^2}{c^2}\).

  • Markov with a strict inequality on an integer variable: \(P(X\gt 50)=P(X\ge51)\le\frac{\mu}{51}\).
  • Chebyshev needs a symmetric interval around μ. "Between 5 and 15" with μ = 10 means \(|X-10|\lt5\). If the interval is not symmetric, use the half-width of the largest symmetric interval inside it.
  • "At least 130 or at most 70 heads in 200 fair tosses": μ = 100, σ² = 50, \(P(|X-100|\ge30)\le50/900=1/18\).
  • Chebyshev with k ≤ 1 gives a useless bound (≥ 1). The quiz still asks for it: answer 1.
  • Find n so that a Chebyshev bound on \(P(|\bar X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\) is below δ: \(\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\lt\delta\) ⇒ \(n\gt\frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2\delta}\).
04L4.1–L4.7
Continuous random variables

Week 4 opens on Seek around 23 Oct. These notes follow the Week 4 slides (already in the course's slides folder) and the Jun 2026 run's Week 4 lectures and assignments. Both recent Quiz 1 papers had two to four Week 4 items worth 6–8 marks.

1. Cumulative distribution function (CDF)

Definition

\(F_X(x)=P(X\le x)\) for every real x. It is non-decreasing, goes to 0 as \(x\to-\infty\), goes to 1 as \(x\to\infty\), and is right-continuous.

  • \(P(a\lt X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)\). Learn this exact form: left end open, right end closed.
  • \(P(X\gt a)=1-F(a)\); \(P(X=a)\) = size of the jump of F at a; \(P(X\lt a)=F(a^-)\) (the value just before a).
  • Discrete X: F is a staircase. The jump points are the range of X and the jump sizes are the PMF. Between jumps F is flat, so \(P(X\le 1.5)=F(1)\) if nothing lies in (1, 1.5].
  • Geometric(p): \(F(x)=1-(1-p)^k\) for \(k\le x\lt k+1\).
  • For \(Y=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\): \(F_Y(y)=F_X(\mu+y\sigma)\).
Example

\(F(x)=0\) for x < 0; 0.3 for 0 ≤ x < 2; 0.75 for 2 ≤ x < 5; 1 for x ≥ 5. Then the range is {0, 2, 5}, \(P(X=2)=0.45\), and \(P(X=5\mid X\ge2)=\frac{0.25}{0.7}\approx0.357\).

2. Continuous and mixed random variables

Definition

X is continuous if its CDF is continuous at every x. Then \(P(X=x)=0\) for every x, and \(P(a\le X\le b)=P(a\lt X\le b)=P(a\le X\lt b)=P(a\lt X\lt b)=F(b)-F(a)\).

  • A CDF with both slopes and jumps is a mixed random variable: the jumps carry point probabilities. E.g. \(F(x)=0.5+0.1x\) on [0, 5] has \(P(X=0)=0.5\).
  • "Which cannot be the CDF of a continuous random variable?" → look for a jump (a break where the two pieces do not meet) or a CDF that does not reach 1.
  • Continuous models: heights, weights, waiting times, rainfall, stock prices. They approximate discrete variables with huge ranges (e.g. Binomial with large n).

3. Probability density function (PDF)

Definition

X has PDF \(f_X\) if \(F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(u)\,du\). Wherever F is differentiable, \(f_X(x)=F_X'(x)\). Probabilities are areas: \(P(a\lt X\lt b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx\).

  • Valid density: \(f(x)\ge0\) everywhere and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1\) (and piecewise continuous). \(\frac4{15}x^3\) on [−1, 2] fails because it is negative for x < 0.
  • A PDF value can be bigger than 1. It is a density, not a probability.
  • \(P(x_0-\varepsilon\lt X\lt x_0+\varepsilon)\approx 2\varepsilon f(x_0)\): a higher density means values near \(x_0\) are more likely. "\(f(2)=2f(1)\) ⇒ probability near 2 ≈ twice that near 1" is true only approximately.
  • Support = \(\{x: f(x)\gt0\}\).
  • Finding k: integrate the PDF over its support and set it to 1. \(k(6-x)\) on (0, 6): \(k\cdot18=1\Rightarrow k=\frac1{18}\).
  • Median / percentile: solve \(F(m)=0.5\) (or 0.75 for the 75th percentile). For \(4x^3\) on [0, 1], \(F=x^4\), median \(=0.5^{1/4}\approx0.841\).
  • Conditional on an event: \(P(X\gt2\mid X\gt1)=\frac{P(X\gt2)}{P(X\gt1)}\), since {X > 2} lies inside {X > 1}.
  • Integrals you need: \(\int x^n dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\), \(\int e^{-ax}dx=-\frac{e^{-ax}}{a}\). Areas of triangles and trapezoids often replace integrals for straight-line PDFs.
Example

\(f(x)=x/18\) on [0, 6]. Then \(F(x)=x^2/36\), \(P(X\gt3)=1-\frac9{36}=\frac34\), \(P(X\gt2)=\frac{32}{36}=\frac89\), and \(P(X\gt3\mid X\gt2)=\frac{3/4}{8/9}=\frac{27}{32}\approx0.844\).

4. Common continuous distributions

NamePDFCDFMeanVariance
Uniform[a, b]\(\frac1{b-a}\), a < x < b\(\frac{x-a}{b-a}\)\(\frac{a+b}2\)\(\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}\)
Exp(λ)\(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\), x > 0\(1-e^{-\lambda x}\)\(\frac1\lambda\)\(\frac1{\lambda^2}\)
Normal(μ, σ²)\(\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\)no closed form\(\mu\)\(\sigma^2\)
  • Uniform: probability = length of the overlap ÷ (b − a). Conditioning a uniform on a sub-interval gives a uniform on that sub-interval: \(P(X\lt10\mid X\lt20)=\frac{10}{20}\) for Uniform[0, 50].
  • Exponential: \(P(X\gt x)=e^{-\lambda x}\), \(P(a\lt X\lt b)=e^{-\lambda a}-e^{-\lambda b}\). "Mean 10 minutes" means λ = 0.1. Memoryless: \(P(X\gt s+t\mid X\gt s)=P(X\gt t)\).
  • Normal: bell curve centred at μ (the most likely region), symmetric. Standardise: \(P(X\le x)=F_Z\!\big(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\big)\). Symmetry: \(F_Z(-z)=1-F_Z(z)\). Normal(2, 5) means variance 5, so σ = √5.
  • Rules of thumb: \(P(|Z|\le1)\approx0.683\), \(P(|Z|\le2)\approx0.954\), \(P(|Z|\le3)\approx0.997\); \(F_Z(1)\approx0.841\), \(F_Z(-1)\approx0.159\).
  • "Replace at most 15.8% under warranty" with lifetime Normal(μ, σ²) and \(F_Z(-1)=0.158\) ⇒ warranty = μ − σ.
Example

\(X\sim\) Normal(20, 9). Find \(P(14\lt X\lt 23)\) using \(F_Z\).

Show working

σ = 3, so \(P=F_Z(\frac{23-20}3)-F_Z(\frac{14-20}3)=F_Z(1)-F_Z(-2)\approx0.8413-0.0228=0.8186\).
